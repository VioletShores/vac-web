Violet Shores · the origin

A protocol that emerged from building — not from designing.

Most protocols are designed in whitepapers and then go looking for a problem. This one went the other way: it surfaced, piece by piece, from twenty-five years of making critical infrastructure trustworthy — and from actually building with AI every day until the missing layer became impossible to ignore.

The builder's arc

Rob Zagarella is an engineer before he is anything else. The through-line of his career is a single question asked of different systems: how do you make infrastructure that strangers can rely on? He authored patented smart-grid control technology, led GE's Smart Grid product lines across Asia-Pacific, worked the Silicon Valley chapter at Grid Net — backed by GE Capital, Intel and Cisco — and then founded NNNCo, building it from a Sydney standing start into a global IoT network company deployed across nineteen countries, and serving on the board of the LoRa Alliance, the global standards body for LoRaWAN.

Utilities, cities, national infrastructure: two and a half decades of systems where "trust me" is never an acceptable answer — where the network has to be able to prove what it did.

The question that would not go away

Building intensively with AI through 2025 and 2026 — orchestrating multiple frontier models on real legal, patent and engineering work every day — two absences kept surfacing.

First: when an AI system acts on something that matters, nothing links that action back to a live, verified human being with enforceable bounds. Logins prove a credential was used, not that a person was present. Courts already treat this as the question — an Australian appellate court held years ago that a computer's output is not a valid decision without a human's mental process bound to it, and the AI-agent cases now arriving put a clock on the same gap.

Second: nobody could say, with evidence, which AI model deserved trust for which kind of question. Every claim was vibes, benchmarks-of-the-week, or marketing.

When AI acts — who authorised it? And which AI should you have asked in the first place? Both questions needed measurable answers.

Emerged, phase by phase

The answers weren't designed; they were forced into existence by daily use. An identity problem (prove a real, present person — not a bot, not a recording) became a multi-modal biometric root. A trust problem became a cryptographic authority chain — every agent's power scoped, bounded, and traceable to a verified person, every consequential decision sealed as an independently verifiable record. A reliability problem became a known-answer challenge harness: hundreds of ground-truth questions, thousands of scored challenges, a per-domain trust calibration across every major model — because "which model is best" should be an empirical claim.

Then the discovery underneath: these weren't separate tools. Verified human authority at the root, bounded delegation through the chain, evidence-calibrated intelligence at the edge — a protocol, found rather than invented, proven by the only method that counts: using it to build itself. Every patent drafted, every page shipped, every decision sealed runs through the same rails it describes.

The proving has been deliberately unkind. The biometric ceremony has been field-hardened on moving trains, through Alpine tunnel dropouts, on roaming cellular across borders — every failure mode named, fixed, and made honest. A decision sealed from a train seat, verifiable by anyone, is the standard the system holds itself to.

Where it stands

Working end-to-end today: live biometric verification, sealed Ed25519 decisions with independently checkable audit trails, bounded agent delegation, and a calibration corpus of 440 known-answer questions across 32 domains — more than ten thousand scored challenges.

On the record: 1,000+ patent claims filed; two Internet-Drafts before the IETF (a verified-human-root and an autonomy governor); designed to compose with national e-ID infrastructure and to support the EU AI Act's human-oversight and record-keeping obligations.

The full engineering record — every phase, every failure, every fix — is maintained continuously as the project's technical biography. This page is the short version; the demo is the proof: walk a sealed decision end-to-end, or read the calibration data.
