The EU AI Act asks one hard question: can you prove human oversight?

The clock: from 2 August 2026, the Act's high-risk-system obligations apply in earnest — effective human oversight, automatic event logging, transparency to users — with penalties for non-compliance reaching €15M or 3% of global turnover. Most organisations can describe their oversight. Almost none can prove it.

What the Act actually demands

  • ART 14 Human oversight — high-risk AI must be designed so natural persons can effectively oversee it: understand it, intervene, override, or stop it. Oversight that exists on paper but not in evidence is precisely what enforcement will test.
  • ART 12 Record-keeping — automatic recording of events over the system's lifetime, sufficient to trace how outputs were produced and reviewed.
  • ART 13 Transparency — deployers and affected persons must be able to understand and interpret the system's operation.
  • ART 26 Deployer duties — assign oversight to persons with competence, training, and authority. Authority is the Act's own word — and an unverifiable authority assignment is an unprovable one.

The gap in most compliance stacks

The market is racing to generate audit trails of what AI systems did — logs of outputs, changes, and deployments. Necessary, and not sufficient: the Act's oversight articles turn on who — which competent, authorised natural person oversaw the decision, with what actual authority, and can that be demonstrated to a regulator without asking them to trust your own database. A platform log that says "user jsmith approved" proves an account clicked. It does not prove a person oversaw.

How this platform answers, article by article

  • Oversight by a verified natural person SHIPPED — a live multi-modal ceremony (face, gesture, spoken challenge, bound in real time) establishes that a specific human — not a credential — exercised the oversight moment. Cloned voices and replayed video don't pass.
  • Records that don't require trusting the operator SHIPPED — every overseen decision seals into a tamper-evident accountability record; any holder of the receipt verifies it against the chain independently. Article 12's logs, upgraded to third-party-provable evidence.
  • Authority, not just identity SHIPPED — capability-narrowing delegation means the record shows the overseer held the authority for that class of decision — Article 26's competence-and-authority requirement made checkable.
  • Regulation as configuration IN DEVELOPMENT — the Act enters the system as a versioned rails pack: its requirements compiled into hard policy floors the platform's controller can never relax. When the regulation updates, compliance updates as content, not as a rebuild — and the same machinery holds the next regime, or a sovereign department's own rails.
  • Oversight that scales with risk IN DEVELOPMENT — work-aware posture: the orchestration layer classifies the risk of what's being done and tightens assurance mid-session, tighten-only, with the reason always shown. High-risk moments meet high-assurance oversight automatically.
  • Transparency one tap away IN DEVELOPMENT — the provenance view extends from "which model and why" to "what oversight applied and why": posture, rails, and the human authority chain readable from the conversation itself.
